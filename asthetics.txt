# THE BEENISH EXPEDITION
## Complete Visual Design System, Art Direction & Graphics Production Brief

**Project type:** Personal interactive birthday adventure  
**Visual genre:** Premium indie pixel-art adventure × cozy fantasy × playful retro-computer interface  
**Emotional direction:** Wonder, curiosity, affection, humor, nostalgia, and quiet intimacy  
**Primary subject:** Beenish  
**Core concept:** An entire little universe has been created for one person. It begins incomplete, mysterious, and almost asleep. As Beenish explores it, she discovers places, artifacts, jokes, memories, and small pieces of a world that gradually becomes brighter, warmer, and more alive.

---

# 01. THE CREATIVE VISION

## 1.1 What we are creating

We are not creating a conventional birthday website, a romantic scrapbook, a generic visual novel, or a standard retro game landing page.

We are creating **a small, explorable fictional world dedicated to Beenish**.

The website should feel like a playable memory: part indie game, part interactive storybook, part secret world, and part love letter.

The visitor should feel that she has discovered something unusual rather than simply opened a webpage.

The experience begins in a mysterious, nighttime world. The player encounters an unusual mission, discovers that the world has somehow lost its stars, and begins exploring different places to recover the things that make the universe beautiful.

Each location has a distinct personality. Some are funny. Some are visually spectacular. Some reveal real photographs or memories. Some contain little interactive surprises. One eventually leads to a peaceful garden where the adventure becomes quieter and more personal.

The world itself should communicate the story without requiring paragraphs of explanation.

**The central visual idea: the more Beenish discovers, the more beautiful the world becomes.**

At first, the world is dark, quiet, and incomplete. As she explores, lights turn on, flowers bloom, stars return, and small creatures appear. By the end, the same world feels alive, warm, and full of color.

This transformation must be visible, gradual, and satisfying.

## 1.2 The intended reaction

We want the experience to produce a sequence of reactions:

1. **Curiosity:** "What is this?"
2. **Surprise:** "Wait, someone actually made this?"
3. **Amusement:** "What is wrong with this website?"
4. **Recognition:** "This is so specifically about me."
5. **Wonder:** "There is actually more to discover."
6. **Affection:** "He put so much thought into this."
7. **Emotional intimacy:** "This feels like something that belongs to us."

These reactions must come from the visual experience, not simply from romantic text.

A tiny character waving from a distant hill, a ridiculous official document about Beenish's beauty, a secret room containing an actual memory, or a flower that blooms when she approaches can communicate more personality than another generic heart animation.

## 1.3 The most important design principle

**Every visual should either build the world, reveal personality, support exploration, or strengthen the emotional connection.**

Avoid adding decorations merely because they are conventionally associated with romance.

A pink heart can be appropriate. Fifty floating pink hearts are not a design system.

A star can represent a recovered memory. A star that floats around the screen for no reason is visual clutter.

A pixel character can make the experience feel personal. A random collection of generic pixel characters will make it feel like a template.

The website must feel deliberately art-directed from beginning to end.

# 02. THE MASTER AESTHETIC

## 2.1 The visual identity

The overall aesthetic is:

**Midnight pixel-art fantasy with soft atmospheric lighting, cozy environments, tiny playful details, and a carefully designed retro-computer interface.**

Imagine the visual craftsmanship of a small independent adventure game, combined with the intimacy of an illustrated storybook.

The world should be pixelated, but not harsh, ugly, or excessively blocky.

It should be detailed enough to feel handcrafted while remaining visually calm and readable.

Use deliberate pixel clusters, carefully shaped silhouettes, layered scenery, atmospheric depth, and restrained lighting effects.

The interface should feel like an artifact from the same fictional universe rather than a modern website placed on top of a game background.

### The five visual pillars

**A. Midnight atmosphere**

Deep blue night skies, indigo shadows, distant mountains, tiny windows glowing in the distance, scattered stars, quiet clouds, and soft pools of light.

**B. Handcrafted pixel art**

Distinctive environments, recognizable silhouettes, expressive characters, detailed little objects, and intentional pixel placement.

**C. Warmth hidden inside darkness**

The night should never feel threatening or depressing. Warm lights, lavender clouds, peach-colored windows, tiny flowers, and glowing objects should create a sense of safety.

**D. Playful absurdity**

Official-looking documents for ridiculous subjects, tiny bureaucratic creatures, impossible museums, absurd warning signs, and characters that react dramatically to ordinary events.

**E. Emotional restraint**

The most sentimental moments should be visually quieter than the funny ones. Use space, lighting, stillness, and a carefully composed scene instead of overwhelming the screen with hearts and effects.

## 2.2 Visual references and interpretation

Use the following references as **directional inspiration, not templates to copy**.

- Stardew Valley — approachable environments, charming environmental details, expressive pixel characters, and a world that feels inhabited.
- Celeste — atmospheric backgrounds, expressive environments, and the ability to communicate emotion through scenery.
- Eastward — richly layered pixel-art locations, cinematic compositions, and a carefully controlled palette.
- Undertale — personality-driven interactions, unexpected humor, and characters whose behavior makes the world memorable.
- To the Moon — intimate storytelling, nostalgic atmosphere, and emotional meaning attached to small details.

Do not imitate any one game's characters, recognizable locations, interface, or signature artwork.

The desired result is an original visual identity that combines atmospheric pixel-art environments, cozy nighttime fantasy, playful interactions, and personal storytelling.

# 03. COLOR SYSTEM

The palette should be limited and intentional.

Most of the world uses cool midnight colors. Warm colors appear as points of interest: windows, lanterns, flowers, collectibles, personal artifacts, and important interactions.

As the story progresses, the world gradually gains more color.

## 3.1 Primary palette

| Color name | Hex | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Void Navy | `#101329` | Deepest background, night sky, dark UI surfaces |
| Midnight Indigo | `#1B2042` | Main environmental shadows and large backgrounds |
| Twilight Blue | `#30385F` | Mountains, buildings, foreground scenery |
| Muted Periwinkle | `#666FA0` | Distant structures, outlines, secondary UI details |
| Dusty Lavender | `#9588BD` | Clouds, flowers, magical elements, soft highlights |
| Dream Lilac | `#C3A9E8` | Magical objects, important highlights, selected states |
| Moon Cream | `#F4EBDD` | Main readable text, stars, soft highlights |
| Warm Peach | `#F3B79C` | Windows, cheeks, flowers, affectionate details |
| Rose Glow | `#E98FAF` | Selective romantic accents and special discoveries |
| Lantern Gold | `#F6D78B` | Collectibles, rewards, lights, recovered stars |
| Moss Green | `#718C83` | Grass, plants, garden scenery |
| Deep Forest | `#354E50` | Dark vegetation, garden shadows |

These are the master colors, not a requirement that every asset use every color.

Each scene should use a small subset of the palette.

## 3.2 Color distribution

As a starting point, use approximately:

- 50% deep navy and midnight indigo.
- 20% blue, periwinkle, and muted lavender.
- 15% dark environmental colors, including forest tones.
- 10% warm cream and peach.
- 5% vivid accents such as gold, rose, and bright lilac.

These proportions are guidelines, not strict measurements.

The opening should be predominantly dark. The museum may use more lavender and warm cream. The observatory should emphasize indigo and celestial gold. The final garden should use the richest combination of the palette.

The world should become more colorful through the story without abruptly changing into a completely different visual style.

## 3.3 Color rules

1. Never use pure black as the dominant background when midnight navy can provide more atmosphere.
2. Avoid large areas of saturated neon pink.
3. Avoid generic pink-and-red Valentine's Day palettes.
4. Avoid using gradients everywhere.
5. Keep important objects brighter than their surroundings.
6. Use warm light to guide attention.
7. Use cool shadows to create depth.
8. Keep text readable against every background.
9. Reserve the brightest gold for discoveries, important objects, and significant moments.
10. Let the final garden feel warmer and more colorful than any earlier location.

### Lighting behavior

Lights should appear to come from actual objects.

A lantern illuminates the nearby ground. A window casts a small patch of warm light. A recovered star adds a subtle glow to its surroundings.

Do not apply a uniform glow to every object.

The lighting should make the environment feel physical, even though the artwork is pixel-based.

# 04. PIXEL-ART STYLE SPECIFICATION

This section is especially important because inconsistent pixel art can make an otherwise beautiful website look like a collection of unrelated assets.

## 4.1 Pixel density

Use a consistent pixel-art grid.

Recommended approach:

- Design core environment artwork at an internal resolution between approximately 320 × 180 and 480 × 270 for widescreen scenes.
- Scale artwork up using integer scaling wherever practical.
- Use a consistent pixel scale within each scene.
- Use larger pixel clusters for distant scenery.
- Use smaller, more detailed clusters for foreground objects and characters.
- Avoid combining extremely coarse sprites with highly detailed sprites in the same visual plane.

The exact internal resolution may vary depending on the complexity of the illustration.

The important requirement is visual consistency.

Do not create one environment with enormous square pixels and another with nearly photographic detail.

## 4.2 Edges and outlines

Use deliberate, clean pixel edges.

Characters and important objects may use dark indigo outlines to remain readable against busy backgrounds.

Background objects should generally have softer contrast and less prominent outlines.

Avoid thick black outlines around every single object.

Not every asset needs an outline. Atmospheric backgrounds may use color separation and lighting instead.

## 4.3 Pixel clusters

Pixel art should be constructed from purposeful clusters rather than random individual pixels.

For example:

- Hair should have a recognizable silhouette and a few deliberate highlight clusters.
- Flowers should have readable petals, stems, and leaves.
- Clouds should have distinct stepped silhouettes.
- Windows should use small, controlled blocks of warm light.
- Water should use horizontal pixel clusters rather than noisy random dots.
- Fabric should have simple color groupings instead of excessive texture.

Individual pixels should contribute to shape, texture, lighting, or expression.

Avoid random pixel noise used merely to make an image look more detailed.

## 4.4 Texture

Use texture selectively.

Appropriate textures include:

- Small stars in the sky.
- Sparse grass clusters.
- Stepped mountain edges.
- Tiny flowers.
- Wooden floorboards.
- Bookshelves.
- Stone paths.
- Pixelated clouds.
- Small reflections on water.
- Occasional glowing particles.

Do not cover the entire scene with noise, grain, sparkles, or decorative pixels.

The background must remain visually calm enough for interactive objects and text to stand out.

## 4.5 Atmospheric depth

Build scenes using at least three visual depth layers when the composition benefits from them:

1. **Background:** sky, moon, distant mountains, stars, distant buildings.
2. **Middle ground:** buildings, trees, paths, platforms, large environmental objects.
3. **Foreground:** grass, flowers, stones, furniture, small props, and objects the character can approach.

Use darker and less detailed silhouettes for distant elements.

Use greater contrast and more recognizable details for foreground objects.

The resulting environment should feel like a place with depth rather than a flat illustration.

## 4.6 Pixel art versus interface rendering

The environmental artwork should be pixel art.

The website interface may use modern CSS for borders, layout, transitions, and readable typography, but its visual language must remain consistent with the world.

For example, an interactive panel might use a crisp rectangular frame, pixel-style corners, a small pixel icon, and a subtle dark background.

It should not look like a generic modern SaaS dashboard floating over pixel scenery.

Likewise, do not pixelate the actual HTML text until it becomes difficult to read.

**The world is pixelated. The reading experience remains comfortable.**

# 05. TYPOGRAPHY

Typography must balance retro personality with readability.

Do not use one pixel font for everything.

## 5.1 Three typography roles

### Role A — Adventure headings

Use a bold, slightly pixel-inspired display font.

It should feel like the title screen of a charming indie game.

Use it for:

- THE BEENISH EXPEDITION.
- Location names.
- Major discoveries.
- Chapter titles.
- Important mission announcements.

The font should be expressive without looking childish or excessively arcade-like.

### Role B — Computer interface

Use a clean monospaced font for the fictional operating system, logs, status indicators, file names, and mission labels.

Examples include:

- `EXPLORER STATUS: ACTIVE`
- `LOCATION: UNKNOWN`
- `RECOVERED STARS: 03 / 07`
- `MISSION CLASSIFICATION: EXTREMELY PERSONAL`

Keep the interface text compact and organized.

### Role C — Personal writing

Use a highly readable humanist sans-serif or soft serif for letters, memory descriptions, personal messages, and longer passages.

The emotional writing should feel handwritten in spirit, even when it is typeset.

Avoid using pixel fonts for long romantic paragraphs.

## 5.2 Font suggestions

Possible starting points:

- Silkscreen for short pixel-inspired headings.
- IBM Plex Mono for interface text.
- Nunito Sans for readable personal content.

These are suggestions, not mandatory dependencies. Choose fonts that can be legally embedded and rendered reliably across browsers.

## 5.3 Typography rules

- Use the display font sparingly.
- Keep body text at a comfortable reading size.
- Avoid extremely thin text against dark backgrounds.
- Avoid excessive uppercase text in emotional passages.
- Use monospaced typography for intentional interface elements, not every paragraph.
- Use generous line spacing for personal letters.
- Avoid decorative typography that makes the interface difficult to use.
- Ensure long text wraps naturally on mobile screens.

The contrast between playful computer text and warm personal writing is intentional.

It helps distinguish the fictional adventure from the real emotions beneath it.

# 06. THE WORLD'S VISUAL GRAMMAR

The entire experience must follow a consistent set of visual rules.

## 6.1 Rectangular geometry

Pixel art naturally favors stepped shapes, rectangular forms, and deliberate right angles.

Use these shapes throughout:

- Stepped window frames.
- Blocky stone paths.
- Rectangular interface panels.
- Pixelated clouds.
- Layered cliffs.
- Geometric doors.
- Small square collectibles.
- Chunky signboards.
- Pixel-cornered cards.

Do not round every element into the same pill-shaped modern UI style.

Some soft corners are acceptable in personal letters or selected interface panels, but the overall geometry should remain recognizably pixel-inspired.

## 6.2 The recurring star

A small four-point or pixel-cluster star should serve as the central visual symbol of the adventure.

Create a master star design with:

- A recognizable silhouette.
- A small palette of gold and cream.
- One bright highlight.
- Optional subtle glow.
- A clearly defined pixel structure.

This symbol should recur in:

- The title screen.
- Mission indicators.
- Collectible objects.
- Environmental decorations.
- Discovery animations.
- The expedition journal.
- The observatory.
- The final garden.

However, do not stamp the star onto every element.

Its meaning should become stronger as the story progresses.

Initially, stars are missing or dim. Later, the player discovers individual stars. By the end, the sky contains a complete constellation.

## 6.3 Recurring motifs

Use a small family of recognizable motifs:

**The star:** discovery, wonder, and the returning night sky.

**The lantern:** warmth, guidance, and safe places.

**The flower:** the gradual return of life to the world.

**The envelope:** personal messages and memories.

**The tiny key:** secrets and optional discoveries.

**The computer cursor:** playful interaction and the fictional operating system.

**The little companion character:** the creator's presence within the world.

**The constellation:** future dreams and the possibility of new memories.

All these motifs should belong to the same visual language.

# 07. CHARACTER ART DIRECTION

Characters are essential because they give the world emotional presence.

## 7.1 Beenish's character

Create a small, charming pixel-art character representing Beenish.

The character should feel like a protagonist in a cozy indie adventure.

Possible design elements:

- A distinctive silhouette.
- Expressive eyes.
- A recognizable hairstyle.
- A simple outfit with a memorable color combination.
- Small, readable animations.
- A few expressive poses.

However, **do not invent her exact appearance**.

Before creating a personalized sprite, use actual reference photographs or details provided by the creator.

If no reference is available, begin with a neutral placeholder design and clearly identify it as provisional.

Do not assume her hair length, hair color, skin tone, clothing preferences, or other physical features.

## 7.2 The creator's companion character

Create a second, smaller pixel character representing the person who made the adventure.

This character is not the hero of the story. Beenish is.

The companion should behave like a slightly ridiculous guide who has been trying to prepare this world for her.

Possible behaviors:

- Waving from behind a tree.
- Running away after saying something embarrassing.
- Holding a sign with an unnecessarily dramatic message.
- Looking proud after completing a task.
- Accidentally dropping a collectible.
- Appearing in the wrong place.
- Leaving a note and disappearing.
- Sitting quietly in the final garden.

The character should be playful and expressive without constantly interrupting the experience.

## 7.3 Character proportions

Use a consistent sprite design.

A practical starting point is a character around 24–40 pixels tall at the native artwork resolution, depending on the internal scene resolution and desired level of detail.

The character should remain recognizable when scaled up.

Avoid excessive detail that becomes unreadable at small sizes.

## 7.4 Character animation set

Prepare the following states where appropriate:

1. Idle.
2. Walking.
3. Looking around.
4. Waving.
5. Celebrating.
6. Sitting.
7. Blushing or hiding the face.
8. Surprised reaction.
9. Sad or thoughtful idle.
10. Reading a letter.
11. Interacting with an object.
12. Resting in the final garden.

Not every animation must be used immediately.

Build a reusable character system so additional scenes can use the same character without redesigning it.

## 7.5 Animation principles

Animations should be short, expressive, and readable.

Suggested timings:

- Idle movement: approximately 1–3 seconds per cycle.
- Walking: approximately 100–180 milliseconds per frame.
- Small reactions: approximately 200–600 milliseconds.
- Discovery animation: approximately 500–1,200 milliseconds.
- Major scene transition: approximately 400–900 milliseconds.

These are starting points rather than strict requirements.

Use timing that suits the scene.

Avoid continuous exaggerated bouncing, spinning, or shaking.

The characters should feel alive, not hyperactive.

# 08. THE MAIN WORLD MAP

The website should feel like a collection of connected locations in one fictional world.

The locations may be separate screens, illustrated scenes, or interactive environments. They do not all need to be technically complex games.

What matters is that the visual continuity makes them feel like parts of the same universe.

## 8.1 World geography

The world should have a recognizable overall geography.

Suggested layout:

- A central starting area with a computer and a small signpost.
- A path leading toward the Bureau of Unreasonable Boyfriends.
- A museum building farther along the path.
- A courthouse with an absurdly serious appearance.
- An archive or library built into a hillside.
- An observatory on a raised platform or mountain.
- A quiet, partially hidden path leading toward the final garden.

The exact arrangement can change to suit the final composition.

The world should not resemble a generic game level select screen.

Locations should exist naturally within the environment.

## 8.2 The central hub

The central hub is the main place Beenish returns to between discoveries.

It should contain:

- A small computer or mission terminal.
- A signpost with the locations.
- A few buildings visible in the distance.
- A path or clearing.
- One or two decorative trees.
- A small patch of flowers.
- The recurring companion character.
- An open night sky with noticeably few stars.

Initially, the hub should feel quiet and incomplete.

As locations are explored, the environment should change:

- More stars appear in the sky.
- Previously dark windows become illuminated.
- Flowers gradually bloom.
- The computer gains new interface details.
- Small environmental characters become visible.
- The central path becomes more inviting.

These changes should persist when the visitor returns.

The hub becomes a visual record of progress.

# 09. LOCATION-BY-LOCATION VISUAL SPECIFICATION

Each location needs its own identity while remaining consistent with the overall art direction.

## 9.1 Location One: The Bureau of Unreasonable Boyfriends

### Concept

A completely serious government office dedicated to investigating the deeply suspicious phenomenon known as Beenish.

The humor comes from treating absurd romantic observations as official matters of national importance.

### Environment

Create a small pixel-art government office at night.

Visual elements:

- A dark blue exterior.
- A signboard reading `BUREAU OF UNREASONABLE BOYFRIENDS`.
- Warm windows.
- A small entrance.
- A noticeboard.
- A desk.
- Bookshelves and filing cabinets.
- Stacks of paper.
- A slightly crooked framed certificate.
- A tiny desk lamp.
- An unnecessarily large official stamp.
- The companion character working very seriously.

The office should feel cozy, cluttered, and faintly ridiculous.

Avoid making it look like a realistic corporate office.

### Palette

Primarily midnight navy, twilight blue, dusty lavender, moon cream, and warm peach.

### Interactive visual details

- A filing cabinet opens when clicked.
- Documents slide out of folders.
- A stamp lands with a small pixel animation.
- A desk lamp turns on.
- A file briefly displays an exaggerated warning.
- The companion character looks suspiciously proud of the paperwork.

### Documents and graphics

Prepare reusable document templates:

- `CASE_001: THE HAIR INCIDENT`
- `CASE_002: UNREASONABLE INTELLIGENCE`
- `CASE_003: EYES UNDER INVESTIGATION`
- `CASE_004: EXCESSIVE KINDNESS`
- `CASE_005: THE SUBJECT IS BEING TOO CUTE`
- `FINAL_REPORT: OBJECTIVITY COMPROMISED`

These are sample file titles. Use only those that genuinely suit Beenish and the relationship.

The office's humor should reflect real observations, personal jokes, and the creator's affectionate exaggeration.

Do not invent personal habits or inside jokes.

### Visual gag

Include a small framed certificate that reads:

`CERTIFIED: EXTREMELY BIASED`

Underneath it, show the companion character giving a thumbs-up.

The joke should be visible even before the document is opened.

## 9.2 Location Two: The Museum of Beenish

### Concept

A museum dedicated to a person who is, according to its extremely unreliable curators, a phenomenon that science cannot adequately explain.

This location combines personal photographs, real achievements, meaningful qualities, and playful exaggeration.

### Environment

Create a beautiful, slightly surreal museum.

Visual elements:

- A grand entrance with stepped architecture.
- Tall windows.
- A dark blue and lavender exterior.
- Warm interior lighting.
- A central exhibition hall.
- Small display stands.
- Pixel-art picture frames.
- A star-shaped chandelier.
- A staircase or upper gallery.
- A few decorative plants.
- A quiet, spacious layout.

The building should feel special without becoming a royal palace.

### Palette

Deep indigo, dusty lavender, moon cream, dream lilac, warm peach, and restrained gold.

### Exhibits

Each exhibit should look like a real museum display.

Possible examples:

**The Portrait Hall**

Actual photographs of Beenish presented inside carefully designed frames.

**The Achievement Wing**

Real accomplishments presented as miniature exhibits, certificates, medals, books, or display cases.

**The Soft Heart Exhibit**

A display representing her kindness, care for others, and meaningful personal qualities.

**The Unexplainable Phenomenon**

A deliberately ridiculous exhibit suggesting that ordinary laws of reality do not adequately explain her existence.

Use actual facts and creator-provided details.

### Photograph presentation

Photographs should be treated as important objects, not generic image thumbnails.

Each image should have:

- A carefully designed frame.
- A short, personal caption.
- Adequate breathing room.
- A subtle reveal or hover effect.
- A readable full-size view when opened.

Use real photographs wherever possible.

Do not generate artificial substitutes for real photographs.

Do not use AI-generated images pretending to be genuine memories.

### Visual gag

One exhibit should be conspicuously overengineered.

For example, a large display case with warning signs, measuring instruments, and a ridiculous official label:

`SUBJECT EXCEEDS CURRENT UNDERSTANDING`

The exhibit should feel like a loving joke rather than a generic compliment.

## 9.3 Location Three: The Court of Absolutely No Flaws

### Concept

A fictional courtroom where the creator attempts to prove that Beenish has flaws.

The court eventually determines that the investigation is hopelessly biased.

### Environment

Create a small, dramatic courtroom.

Visual elements:

- A raised judge's bench.
- Wooden or dark indigo furniture.
- A large central desk.
- A witness stand.
- Tall windows.
- A small flag or official emblem.
- A ridiculous stack of evidence folders.
- A large wall clock.
- A tiny gavel.
- A sign reading `COURT OF ABSOLUTELY NO FLAWS`.

Use a palette of midnight navy, muted lavender, warm cream, and restrained peach.

The environment should feel theatrical rather than realistic.

### Interactive details

- A gavel makes a short pixel animation.
- A document is presented as evidence.
- A fictional objection appears.
- The judge looks at the evidence.
- The companion character becomes increasingly embarrassed.
- The final verdict appears as a stamped document.

### Visual humor

Make the court look excessively serious about an inherently ridiculous case.

A large stack of empty evidence folders can communicate the joke before the player reads anything.

The visual punchline should be simple and immediately understandable.

Do not create a long, complicated courtroom minigame unless it genuinely improves the experience.

## 9.4 Location Four: The Archive of Things That Happened

### Concept

A quiet archive containing actual memories from the relationship.

This location is the bridge between the fictional adventure and the real relationship.

It should be noticeably more intimate than the earlier locations.

### Environment

Create a nighttime archive or library.

Visual elements:

- Tall bookshelves.
- A narrow central aisle.
- Warm reading lamps.
- Small tables.
- A window showing the night sky.
- A few plants.
- Books and labeled boxes.
- A central memory cabinet.
- Tiny glowing dust particles, used sparingly.
- A comfortable reading chair.

The room should feel peaceful and private.

### Palette

Midnight indigo, dusty lavender, warm cream, muted peach, and small gold accents.

### Memory artifacts

Each real memory should appear as a physical object.

Possible forms:

- A photograph inside a frame.
- A folded letter.
- A small box containing an image.
- A message displayed on an old computer.
- A ticket or note.
- A small illustrated scene based on an actual event.
- A book containing a particular conversation.

Only use memories supplied by the creator.

Do not invent conversations, dates, events, or shared experiences.

If a real photograph or screenshot is used, preserve its authenticity.

### Visual treatment

When a memory is opened:

1. The selected object becomes the visual focus.
2. The background becomes slightly quieter.
3. The memory appears in a readable panel or carefully composed scene.
4. A short caption explains why it matters.
5. The visitor can close it and return to the archive.

Do not turn every memory into a full-screen dramatic presentation.

Some memories should be small and ordinary.

An ordinary conversation can be meaningful without requiring a cinematic treatment.

### Emotional direction

This location should feel like opening a box of things that someone carefully kept because they mattered.

It must not feel like a generic gallery.

## 9.5 Location Five: The Observatory of Things We Haven't Done Yet

### Concept

A beautiful observatory dedicated to future experiences the couple hopes to share.

These are possibilities, not promises or obligations.

### Environment

Create an observatory on a hill overlooking the world.

Visual elements:

- A large telescope.
- A rounded observatory dome.
- A wooden platform.
- A small staircase.
- A star map.
- A few glowing instruments.
- A clear view of the night sky.
- Distant mountains.
- A tiny bench.
- A constellation with several missing stars.

The scene should feel expansive, quiet, and hopeful.

### Palette

Midnight navy, deep indigo, dream lilac, moon cream, and lantern gold.

### Interactive details

Each selected star reveals a future experience.

Possible categories include:

- A photoshoot together.
- Making couple reels.
- Going on dates.
- Taking nighttime walks.
- Visiting places together.
- Creating new memories.

Only include dreams that genuinely reflect the creator's hopes and Beenish's interests.

### Visual representation

Each future experience should have a distinct visual symbol.

For example:

- A small camera for a photoshoot.
- Two tiny figures walking beneath a moon.
- A film frame for making videos.
- A pair of chairs at a café.
- A small road leading toward distant mountains.

These should be original illustrations in the established pixel-art style.

### The unnamed constellation

Include a small constellation that has no name yet.

The interface should invite Beenish to name it herself.

This should be a genuine optional interaction, not a required form.

The visual should communicate that some parts of the story are still unwritten.

## 9.6 The hidden garden

### Concept

The most emotionally intimate location in the entire experience.

The garden should feel like a place that was always part of the world but remained hidden until the visitor discovered it.

### Environment

Create a peaceful pixel-art garden at night.

Visual elements:

- A narrow stone path.
- Soft grass.
- Small flowers.
- A few trees.
- A pond or small reflective water feature.
- Warm lanterns.
- A wooden bench.
- Lavender and peach flowers.
- A few fireflies.
- A deep blue sky.
- A constellation above the garden.
- The two characters sitting or standing together.

The environment should feel secluded, safe, and quiet.

It should not look like a grand fantasy kingdom or a generic romantic castle.

### Palette

This is the location where the palette reaches its warmest expression.

Use:

- Deep forest green.
- Moss green.
- Midnight navy.
- Dusty lavender.
- Dream lilac.
- Warm peach.
- Moon cream.
- Lantern gold.

Allow the warm colors to appear more frequently than in earlier locations.

### Lighting

Lanterns should illuminate the path and nearby flowers.

Fireflies should appear as a few small, moving points of light.

The water may reflect the moon or nearby lights.

Flowers may have subtle highlights.

The sky should contain a fuller constellation than the opening scene.

Avoid intense bloom effects, excessive particles, and giant glowing hearts.

### Character composition

The two characters should be positioned together naturally.

Possible compositions:

- Sitting on a bench beneath a tree.
- Standing near a pond.
- Looking at the constellation.
- Sitting quietly beside a lantern.
- Standing on a small hill overlooking the world.

Choose one strong composition rather than filling the scene with multiple competing focal points.

### The letter

The final letter should be presented as a real reading experience.

Possible treatments:

- A letter inside a small envelope.
- A note resting on the bench.
- A softly illuminated book.
- A paper object held by the companion character.

The text itself should remain readable and should not be baked into the illustration.

The artwork should frame the letter rather than compete with it.

### The emotional rule

Do not make the garden visually loud.

The most important moment should feel like the world has finally stopped rushing.

The visitor has explored, laughed, and discovered things. Now she can simply sit in the world and read.

Every interaction should have visual feedback.

- Books.
- Photographs.
- Cabinets.
- Stars.
- Flowers.
- Letters.
- Computer terminals.
- Small creatures.
- Hidden keys.
- Collectible artifacts.

Objects should not all behave identically.

A door opens. A book reveals a memory. A star produces a discovery animation. A creature reacts. A letter opens a reading view.

## 12.2 Hover behavior

On desktop, hovering over an interactive object may cause:

- A small brightness change.
- A subtle outline.
- A slight vertical movement.
- A tiny sparkle.
- A short tooltip.
- A character reaction.

Choose the effect that best communicates the interaction.

## 12.3 Click behavior

When an object is selected:

1. Provide immediate visual feedback.
2. Show the result clearly.
3. Preserve the environment where practical.
4. Offer an obvious way to close or return.
5. Avoid unexpected navigation.
6. Avoid making the user wait for unnecessary animation.

## 12.4 Discovery animations

When a collectible star is found:

- The star briefly brightens.
- It rises or moves toward the inventory.
- A small sound may play if sound is enabled.
- The discovery counter updates.
- A subtle visual change occurs in the world.

The animation should be satisfying but brief.

Do not turn each discovery into a long celebration.

## 12.5 Optional secrets

Hide a small number of optional discoveries throughout the world.

Examples:

- A tiny key behind a tree.
- A strange note inside a filing cabinet.
- A small flower that reacts when clicked.
- A companion character hiding behind a building.
- An unusual object in the museum.
- A secret path toward the garden.

Secrets should reward curiosity.

They should never prevent the visitor from reaching the important emotional content.

**The love letter must not be locked behind difficult puzzles or a completion requirement.**

# 13. MOTION AND ANIMATION SYSTEM

Motion should make the world feel alive without making it exhausting.

## 13.1 Environmental motion

Appropriate ambient animations include:

- Stars twinkling occasionally.
- Clouds moving slowly.
- Grass moving slightly.
- Fireflies drifting.
- Water reflections shifting.
- Lanterns flickering subtly.
- Leaves moving gently.
- A character blinking.
- A window light turning on.
- Flowers swaying.

These should be subtle and staggered.

Not every element should move at once.

## 13.2 Motion hierarchy

Use three levels of animation.

### Level 1: Ambient

Very subtle and continuous.

Examples: distant clouds, occasional stars, gentle lantern flicker.

### Level 2: Interactive

Triggered by a user action.

Examples: opening a door, selecting a photograph, clicking a star.

### Level 3: Story events

Used sparingly for significant moments.

Examples: the first recovered star, the return of color to a location, or the reveal of the final garden.

The largest animations should correspond to meaningful events.

## 13.3 Transition style

Prefer transitions that resemble movement through an illustrated world.

Examples:

- A short fade to midnight blue.
- A scene sliding gently into view.
- A door opening to reveal another location.
- A star moving toward the sky.
- A lantern illuminating the next area.

Avoid generic spinning loaders and excessive zooming.

## 13.4 Accessibility

Respect reduced-motion preferences.

When reduced motion is enabled:

- Remove unnecessary parallax.
- Replace large transitions with simple fades.
- Reduce continuous particle effects.
- Avoid flashing.
- Keep interaction feedback clear.

The website should remain complete and enjoyable without animations.

# 16. IMAGE-GENERATION RULES

The image-generation process must preserve consistency.

## 16.1 Master reference first

Before generating the full environment, establish the visual system with a small reference pack:

1. One sample landscape.
2. One sample interior.
3. One sample character.
4. One sample environmental prop.
5. One sample interface panel.
6. One sample warm-light scene.
7. One sample final-garden composition.

Review these before producing dozens of assets.

If the style is wrong, correct the reference pack first.

Do not generate the entire world and attempt to repair inconsistent artwork afterward.

## 16.2 Scene generation

For each scene, specify:

- The location.
- The time of day.
- The camera angle.
- The foreground.
- The middle ground.
- The background.
- The dominant colors.
- The light sources.
- The intended mood.
- The approximate pixel density.
- The intended use in the website.
- The objects that must remain unobstructed.

Avoid prompts that merely say "beautiful pixel-art scene."

That instruction is too vague to produce consistent results.

## 16.3 Camera consistency

Use a consistent perspective across environments.

The primary world should favor a side-view or slightly elevated two-dimensional perspective suitable for a cozy adventure.

Buildings, paths, props, and characters should share compatible proportions.

Interior scenes can use a more frontal composition when necessary, but the overall perspective should remain coherent.

Do not combine:

- Isometric buildings.
- Top-down characters.
- Side-view furniture.
- Three-dimensional photorealistic objects.

These styles do not naturally belong together without deliberate art direction.

## 16.4 Background and foreground separation

When possible, generate environment artwork in separate layers.

For example:

- Sky and distant scenery.
- Buildings and large environmental objects.
- Foreground trees and vegetation.
- Interactive props.
- Character sprites.

This allows the website to create small amounts of parallax and interaction without requiring the entire illustration to move as one image.

However, do not split every scene into dozens of layers unnecessarily.

Use layers where they improve composition or interaction.

## 16.5 Text inside generated artwork

**Do not ask the image generator to produce important readable text.**

Image generators may misspell labels, produce inconsistent typography, or distort words.

Generate clean signboards, empty documents, blank display panels, and interface backgrounds.

Render all important text separately in HTML or SVG.

This includes:

- Location names.
- Case-file titles.
- Interface labels.
- Personal letters.
- Museum captions.
- Mission text.
- Buttons.
- Status indicators.

The artwork supplies the visual object. The website supplies the actual text.

This separation is essential.

## 16.6 Real photographs

Use authentic photographs supplied by the creator.

Do not generate AI portraits and present them as real photographs of Beenish.

Do not modify the appearance of her actual photographs unless explicitly requested.

Photographs can be framed or placed within pixel-art environments, but their content should remain authentic.

# 21. RESPONSIVE DESIGN

The experience must work on desktop and mobile.

The visual system should adapt without losing its identity.

## 21.1 Desktop

On larger screens:

- Show the full environment.
- Keep the player character visible.
- Allow a comfortable amount of scenery.
- Place interface panels where they do not obscure important artwork.
- Use subtle parallax where it genuinely improves depth.
- Support keyboard interaction where appropriate.

The world should feel spacious.

## 21.2 Mobile

On mobile:

- Keep the main character and important objects visible.
- Recompose scenes rather than merely shrinking them.
- Move interface panels into suitable positions.
- Make interactive objects large enough to tap.
- Avoid relying on hover.
- Reduce unnecessary background movement.
- Ensure personal letters remain easy to read.
- Prevent horizontal overflow.

A landscape scene may require a different crop or a specially composed portrait version.

Do not assume that the center of a desktop image will always be the correct mobile composition.

## 21.3 Touch interactions

Interactive targets should generally be at least 44 × 44 CSS pixels where practical.

If an object is visually tiny, provide a larger invisible hit area or a clear contextual control.

Do not require precise tapping on individual pixels.

## 21.4 Mobile art direction

The mobile version should feel intentionally designed, not like a compromised desktop version.

It can use:

- Closer framing.
- Simplified background layers.
- Larger characters.
- More compact navigation.
- Separate scene crops.
- Simplified ambient effects.

Preserve the mood, colors, character identity, and important visual details.

# 22. PERFORMANCE AND VISUAL QUALITY

Pixel art can be visually rich without making the website slow.

## 22.1 Asset optimization

- Use appropriately sized backgrounds.
- Compress photographs carefully.
- Avoid loading every scene at the beginning.
- Load detailed environments when needed.
- Reuse common sprites and interface assets.
- Avoid oversized transparent images containing mostly empty space.
- Use sprite sheets where they are beneficial.
- Keep animation work modest.
- Avoid unnecessary real-time rendering.

## 22.2 Rendering quality

Pixel artwork must remain crisp.

Where integer scaling is practical, use it.

When CSS scaling is used, ensure the intended pixel-art rendering is preserved.

Do not allow accidental blur caused by fractional scaling.

Photographs and typography should remain sharp and natural.

## 22.3 Visual stability

Reserve space for backgrounds, characters, and panels so that loading assets does not cause major layout shifts.

The page should not jump around as the scene loads.

A short, designed transition is preferable to showing broken images or a blank screen.

# 23. WHAT TO AVOID

The following are explicit design restrictions.

## 23.1 Avoid generic romance

Do not fill the website with:

- Hundreds of floating hearts.
- Generic pink gradients.
- Rose petals on every screen.
- Repetitive "I love you" decorations.
- Standard scrapbook layouts.
- Generic romantic stock illustrations.
- Generic couple silhouettes.
- Identical heart-shaped cards.

Romance should emerge through the personal details and the experience itself.

## 23.2 Avoid generic game design

Do not use:

- Generic RPG inventory screens without a story purpose.
- Random coins and gems.
- Unrelated fantasy weapons.
- Generic level-selection maps.
- Arbitrary health bars.
- Combat mechanics.
- Point systems that trivialize emotional content.
- Unnecessary difficulty.
- Repetitive collectible animations.

The experience is an adventure, but it is not a conventional combat game.

## 23.3 Avoid inconsistent artwork

Do not mix:

- Pixel art with photorealistic generated backgrounds.
- Different pixel densities without a clear reason.
- Multiple incompatible character designs.
- Random visual styles from different prompts.
- Unrelated color palettes.
- Excessive gradients and bloom.
- Detailed 3D objects inside flat pixel-art environments.

## 23.4 Avoid emotional overproduction

Do not make every scene dramatic.

Do not use cinematic lighting for every interaction.

Do not make every memory a major revelation.

Do not force the visitor to complete every objective.

Do not make affection conditional on collecting all the stars.

The most meaningful moments should have room to breathe.

# 24. FINAL ART-DIRECTION CHECKLIST

Before accepting any generated asset, evaluate it against the following criteria.

### Style consistency

- Does it look like it belongs to THE BEENISH EXPEDITION?
- Does it use the established palette?
- Is the pixel density consistent?
- Are the edges deliberate and crisp?
- Does the perspective match the rest of the world?
- Does the lighting follow the established rules?

### Composition

- Is the main subject immediately recognizable?
- Is there a clear focal point?
- Is there enough negative space?
- Can interactive objects be identified?
- Will the scene still work when interface elements are added?
- Can the composition be adapted for mobile?

### Atmosphere

- Does the scene feel mysterious but safe?
- Does it feel handcrafted?
- Is the visual detail controlled?
- Are warm and cool colors balanced?
- Does the scene have a distinct personality?

### Story relevance

- Does the asset serve a specific purpose?
- Does it support the location's identity?
- Does it contribute to exploration, humor, memory, or emotional connection?
- Does it avoid introducing unrelated fantasy elements?

### Technical quality

- Are edges clean?
- Are important details readable at the intended size?
- Is text kept separate from generated artwork?
- Is transparency correct where required?
- Is the image appropriately sized?
- Can the asset be reused or revised?

If an asset fails several of these checks, revise it rather than accepting it simply because the image looks attractive in isolation.

# 26. THE FINAL DESIGN RULE

The most important thing to understand is that this website should not look like someone asked an AI to generate a collection of cute pixel-art images.

It should look like **one artist imagined an entire little universe, established its rules, and then designed every location, object, character, and interface element to belong to that universe**.

The Bureau should look like the Bureau.

The Museum should look like a museum within the same world.

The Archive should feel quieter than the Bureau.

The Observatory should open the world up visually.

The Garden should feel like the natural emotional destination of the entire adventure.

The same star, characters, colors, lighting principles, and visual language should connect everything.

The humor should be specific to Beenish.

The memories should be real.

The future should feel inviting rather than demanding.

And the final scene should feel different from everything that came before it—not because it uses an entirely different art style, but because the world has finally become complete.

**The visitor should leave feeling that she did not simply browse a birthday website. She explored a little world that someone made for her, and nowhere else.**
